Today I built a working tool in one day at Claude Impact Lab, Orange County.

The question: does an affordable home pencil, and who closes the gap?

California law caps what a lower-income family can pay for housing. In Orange County, for a family of four earning about $97,000 a year, that's about $2,400 a month. At this week's rates, that buys a home of about $243,000. Building it costs about $585,000.

That difference is the gap. We built the OCMEE Affordable Homes Program to show how big it is and who can close it:

• A gap calculator built on the state formula and the 2026 income limits
• Rate sensitivity, because one point on the mortgage rate moves the gap by about $20,000 per home
• Document reading: Claude pulls the fields from pay stubs, W-2s and loan papers, and code does the math
• Ways to shrink the gap: rate buydown, land trust, co-buyers, shared equity
• An action plan, a project map, and a program atlas for each family
• A "Get involved" page for money, goods, a car, or time

What I learned:

1. Let the model read, let code count. Claude is great at pulling a number off a messy pay stub. The arithmetic belongs in code you can check.

2. Every number needs a source on the screen. If a family or a funder can't see where a figure came from, they shouldn't trust it.

3. "May fit" is not "approved." When you build for people with little room for error, the wording matters as much as the math.

4. Say the unit every time. "$2,400" sounds like a lot or a little depending on whether it's a month, a year, or one time.

5. Use fake data. We built and demoed with fictional families only. Real documents stay with the nonprofit.

6. One page, no login. Nonprofits don't have IT budgets. If it needs a server, it won't get used.

Thank you to the organizers, the nonprofit partners, and my teammates.
